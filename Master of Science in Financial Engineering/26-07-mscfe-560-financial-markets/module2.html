Lesson 1 — Introducing Stocks & Cryptocurrencies

🔑 Keywords: Stock • Primary Market • Secondary Market • Capital Appreciation • Realized Gain/Loss • Going Public • Cryptocurrency

1.1 The Drawback of Bonds → The Strength of Stocks

Bondholder recap:

  • Contractually guaranteed fixed coupons at specified intervals till maturity (last coupon + principal together).
  • In default, bondholders get paid before equity holders.
  • Principal returned = investment value (barring default).
⚠️ The Drawback Payments are FIXED — no matter how well the company performs, the bondholder gets no share of the upside. Stocks exist to fix exactly this: participating in the company's upside is the strength of stocks.
📘 Definition — Stock A security representing fractional ownership in a corporation. Stockholder (= shareholder) owns a fraction of the company + share of profits + voting rights. Earlier physical certificates; today digital. Another name: Equities (because holders own the equity).
🧮 Fundamental Equation of Accounting
Assets = Liabilities + Equity   ⟹   Equity = Assets − Liabilities
Greater assets / smaller liabilities ⟹ greater equity ⟹ stockholders effectively own more.

Governance chain (representative democracy 🗳️): Stockholders vote → Board of Directors → selects CEO & senior managers → staffing/ops decisions. Most holders own tiny fractions ⟹ negligible individual influence.

2 ways to finance assets: ① Liabilities (borrow / issue bonds) ② Equity (issue shares — can even be used to repay liabilities). Public companies sell shares via exchanges (NASDAQ, NYSE, FTSE…), investors trade via stockbrokers. A rise in value is not guaranteed — it can fall, or be wiped out in bankruptcy.

⭐ "Good company" checklist Productive assets + well-controlled liabilities + good management + projects where revenue > costs ⟹ greater valuation.

Types of Stocks (memorize this terminology!)

Buying a stock — 2 markets

📘 Primary Market Buy directly from issuer at issuance = at the IPO. IPO is underwritten by investment banks / broker-dealers — they buy from the company & resell in large blocks ⟹ shares mostly go to large institutional investors (faster/efficient for bankers) — difficult for small investors.
📘 Secondary Market Already-issued shares trade on exchanges. Buy = accept owner's offer price; sell = find willing buyer. Heavy trading ⟹ firm's shares seen as marketable & liquid — a good thing for the company.

1.2 Benefits of Stocks (investor side) — 6 points

  1. Capital Appreciation 💰 — "buy low, sell high"; profit = price difference on sale. Buy undervalued → hold → sell higher = essence of value trading. (The same feeling as selling a house once its price has gone up!)
  2. Dividend Payments — from profits, company either (a) pays dividends proportional to shares, or (b) retains earnings for projects/acquisitions that build long-term value.
  3. Voting Rights — more shares = more votes → board → executives. No voice in day-to-day operations.
  4. Ease of Trading — price publicly known, tradable at/near it during exchange hours (~5 days/week). Easy cash conversion. Contrast: art, real estate — no exchange, valuation hard.
  5. Extra Income via Securities Lending — lend your shares to short sellers via broker; borrower pays a financing cost ("daily rent"). You keep dividends + financing income until short is covered; can still sell meanwhile.
  6. Centralized Depositories — exchanges + clearing houses + custodians keep exact ownership records ⟹ virtually no mix-ups. Contrast real estate: deeds at thousands of local offices; duplicate title claims possible ⟹ which is why title insurance exists. (Note: benefit of exchanges/clearing, not stocks per se.)

Company side — raising capital

Issue Bonds 😰 Raise whatever market supplies, BUT cash-flow obligations start at first coupon date. Funds tied in long-term infra ⟹ default possible as early as the first interest payment. Timing & size of obligations matter.
Issue Stocks 😎 Cede ownership to public — money NEVER has to be paid back. IPO price set by underwriting bank. Safer for long growth cycles (no coupons). Growth phase = no dividends expected. This is "going public" — dilutes founders' % by granting ownership to buyers.

1.3 Disadvantages of Stocks

  • Capital depreciation — a loss while holding is only on paper; selling at a lower price makes it a REALIZED loss. (Key exam term!)
  • Dividends may not occur — no income, or earnings retained instead.
  • Voting power negligible; few/single big holders can control. Activist investors (e.g., Bill Ackman) buy big stakes → pressure management to "shape up or ship out" → adds volatility & uncertainty.
  • Market ≠ 24/7 — bad news while closed ⟹ can't sell for 1–3 days (weekends/holidays) ⟹ risk.
⭐ Bankruptcy payout order (memorize this!)
Secured creditors (incl. bondholders) → Employees → Tax agencies → Environmental claims → Preferred stockholders → Common stockholders (LAST)

Company side: issuing stock can mean a possible loss of control. Ford example: the family held a large voting block (~40% voting power over the board). If an activist acquired enough shares that the family lost control → new board → risk of poor leadership. Bonds avoid this ownership disruption.

1.4 How to Value a Stock

To judge capital appreciation, a fair value is needed. Techniques (corporate-finance rooted):

  • DCF — very general, needs deep company knowledge ⟹ beyond the reach of outsiders.
  • Relative valuation & Dividend Discount Models (DDM) — accessible.
  • Gordon Growth Model — simple yet powerful, no specialized business knowledge needed. ⭐

1.4.1 Gordon Growth Model (GGM)

📘 Idea Present price = sum of all future dividends discounted to today, growing at constant rate in perpetuity (forever). Computed price = fair value. Based on time value of money.
🧮 GGM Formula
P₀ = D₁r − g   where   D₁ = D₀(1 + g),   and must have r > g
Inputs (only 3): D₁ = expected next dividend; g = dividend growth rate; r = cost of equity capital (market's compensation for risk).
Derivation sketch: P₀ = Σ D₁(1+g)t−1/(1+r)t — geometric series, ratio (1+g)/(1+r) < 1 ⟹ sums to D₁/(r−g).
⭐ Decision Rule Market price < GGM value ⟹ undervalued → BUY  |  Market price > GGM value ⟹ overvalued → SELL / short
✏️ Solved Example 1 ABC: D₁ = $1.50, g = 2%, r = 8%. Current price $30.
P₀ = 1.50 / (0.08 − 0.02) = 1.50 / 0.06 = $25.00
Fair $25 < market $30 ⟹ overvalued ⟹ SELL.
✏️ Solved Example 2 (trap! D₀ vs D₁) ABC just paid D₀ = $1.40; g = 2.2%; r = 8%. Formula needs D₁:
D₁ = 1.40 × 1.022 = 1.4308  ⟹  P₀ = 1.4308 / (0.08 − 0.022) = 1.4308 / 0.058 ≈ $24.67
⚠️ Exam Alert "expected to pay" ⟹ use D₁ directly  •  "just paid" ⟹ multiply by (1+g) first! This is a classic quiz trap.

GGM disadvantages (5):

  1. Needs dividend-paying company — stable/mature firms OK; unreliable/inapplicable for start-ups.
  2. Constant g mostly fits mature firms; growth firms' growth is volatile.
  3. Assumes company exists forever, pays & grows dividends forever.
  4. Mathematically needs r > g.
  5. External market conditions ignored.

1.4.2 Multistage GGM (mGGM)

Fast-growth period + stable-growth period (young = fast, mature = slow & stable). Inputs: D₁ (or D₀), fast rate gf, high-growth years n, stable rate gs, r.

🧮 2-stage formula
P₀ = Σt=1..n D₀(1+gf)t(1+r)t + D₀(1+gf)n(1+gs)(r − gs) × 1(1+r)n
= PV of explicit dividends + PV of terminal Gordon value. Easy in a spreadsheet; extends to N stages.

1.4.3 H-Model (linearly declining growth)

3 stages: ① high growth gH (1 period) ② growth declines linearly gH → gL over 2H periods ③ perpetuity at gL.

🧮 Closed form
P₀ = D₀(1 + gL) + D₀ · H · (gH − gL)r − gL    H = (decline years) ÷ 2  (= half-life)
✏️ Solved Example — STU Price $34; r = 9.5%; D₀ = $1.85; gH = 7%; gL = 3%; decline over 10 yrs ⟹ H = 5.
P₀ = [1.85(1.03) + 1.85 × 5 × 0.04] / 0.065 = [1.9055 + 0.37] / 0.065 ≈ $35.01
$35.01 > $34 ⟹ mildly undervalued.

1.4.4 True Multistage H-Model

Stage 1 high growth now lasts n periods (not just 1) → linear decline → perpetuity. Formula is cumbersome ⟹ use a spreadsheet (project each dividend, discount, add terminal value).

✏️ Solved Example — PQR Price $27.14; D₀ = $0.55; 10% growth × 5 yrs, then linear decline to 5% over 10 yrs; r = 8.5% (CAPM). Spreadsheet ⟹ fair value $24.75 ⟹ market overvalued by ≈ 9.6% (27.14/24.75 − 1).

2. Cryptocurrency 🪙

⭐ Timeline (memorize these dates) 1991 — Haber & Stornetta: blockchain technique → 2002 — Back: proof of work → 2008 — Satoshi Nakamoto combines these → Bitcoin (launched as world entered the Great Recession).   2015 — Vitalik Buterin: Ethereum.
📘 Ledger A (possibly digital) book of bookkeeping entries summarizing ALL of a company's financial transactions over a period — a master document.
📘 Blockchain A shared and immutable ledger that records transactions & tracks assets = distributed ledger technology / distributed database. Traditional: central authority controls the DB. Nakamoto: single distributed DB accessible to everyone, controlled by no one, permissionless. Everyone agrees on the DB state ⟹ all copies sync ⟹ saves time & usually cost.

Figure 1 concept: Decentralization is a SPECTRUM — permissioned centralized (few big nodes) → node significance becomes uniform → permissionless decentralized (fully "Decentralized" endpoint = more conceptual than practical).

🧮 7 Blockchain properties (solving the challenges of a permissionless ledger)
  1. All nodes hold a copy of the same ledger
  2. Accepted ledger = immutable; validated transactions irreversible
  3. Transactions time-stamped (no double-adding)
  4. All nodes agree on validity of each block + whole chain (consensus)
  5. Participants anonymous
  6. Everything encrypted
  7. Programmable (needed for smart contracts)

How Bitcoin works ⚙️

Bitcoin's blockchain = shared history of every BTC transaction; copies on network computers = nodes. Nodes validate transactions (does the payer actually have the coins?). Valid transactions → blocks → appended to chain. Node owners = miners; adding a block earns a bitcoin reward — this is how new BTC are created!

⭐ Security = 2 protections ① Hash — cryptographic fingerprint per block; costly to generate (computing + energy) ⟹ proof-of-work; then acts as a seal; verifying is easy. ② Consensus protocol — nodes agree on shared history, then update copies.

Hashes also link the chain (each block contains previous block's hash). To alter an old entry: recompute that hash + every later block's hash, FASTER than the network adds new blocks ⟹ needs more power than all other nodes combined — otherwise there's a conflict → the alteration is rejected. Hard to attack, but not completely unbreakable.

Ethereum & Smart Contracts

  • Ethereum (2015, Vitalik Buterin) — decentralized blockchain running smart contracts: programs that create/transform data or tokens on the chain; control assets/data; define transactions ⟹ basis of DeFi. Native currency: Ether (ETH).
  • ERC standards: ERC-20 = fungible (1 EUR ≡ 1 EUR)  •  ERC-721 = non-fungible (token tied to a particular asset).
  • Consensus: Bitcoin = proof-of-work vs Ethereum = proof-of-stake (any ETH holder can secure the network & earn rewards by batching transactions + checking other validators).
  • dApps — permissionless decentralized applications, no central controller; governed by a DAO (Decentralized Autonomous Organization) with rules encoded in smart contracts.
  • Wallets — apps giving control over accounts (identity, balances, send transactions) = tools for interacting with Ethereum accounts.
📘 Oracle Bridge between the blockchain and the external world — brings off-chain data on-chain (chain only knows on-chain events). Example: soccer-betting dApp needs the true result; malicious actors want to feed false data. Types: centralized, decentralized, consensus-based, human-based — tradeoff: safety vs ease vs simplicity vs cost.

Other cryptos: Tether, Solana, Litecoin, Bitcoin Cash, BNB, Dogecoin… Exchanges: e.g., Coinbase. BTC price can move substantially in a day; smaller coins even more volatile.

💡 Why crypto appeals: stock selection daunting; good company mil bhi jaye toh price already high; shorting stock = UNLIMITED potential loss (price rise without bound) ⟹ some investors choose to pick an entirely different asset class instead. Crypto: transcends borders, no central authority — trust in institutions replaced by "objective, rational, incorruptible mathematical calculations" (Stellinga et al.).

2.1 Crypto Benefits (3)

  1. Extreme returns — far more volatility than stocks; attractive if you're good at market timing.
  2. Exponential growth — didn't exist ~20 yrs ago; now ≥10,000 active; total $1–2 trillion.
  3. 24/7 trading — no physical exchange/closing hours; react to news instantly. Peloton example: short into weekend → takeover news → Monday open +30% → short seller loses 30%. Crypto-style round-the-clock access would've prevented it.

2.2 Crypto Disadvantages

  • No dividends ⟹ stock-valuation mechanisms (DDM etc.) don't apply — hard to judge value.
  • Famous skeptics 🗣️: Warren Buffett — Bitcoin "probably rat poison squared", a gambling instrument; Nassim Taleb (2021) — worth zero, fails as hedge; Jamie Dimon — no intrinsic value, "you are basically buying a token".
  • Only way to profit: someone else later believes it's worth more (→ Greater Fool, Lesson 2). Stocks = companies making products/services; crypto = implied value recognized by holders.
  • Distinguish from real-world currencies = FX (foreign exchange) (e.g., GBP/JPY).

2.3 Bitcoin vs Bonds — return spectrum's 2 ends

🏦 Risk-free bonds Most conservative; principal + interest virtually guaranteed if issuer solvent. Held to maturity ⟹ earn YTM; bought at par ⟹ YTM = coupon rate. Returns modest (small fraction of principal).
🪙 Bitcoin Little/no intrinsic value (no products/services). Value-add: publicly distributed ledger for many applications — but value subjective. $0 → tens of thousands (projected ~$100,000 while notes were written). +50% to +1000s% possible… but losing 90% is equally easy; 40–50% corrections common. Athletes paid in BTC sometimes found much smaller amounts at withdrawal time.

Lesson 2 — Types of Stocks & Cryptocurrencies

🔑 Keywords: Capitalization • Dilution • Private Equity • Price Dynamics • Alternative Investments • Commodities • Art

1. Large Cap vs Small Cap

🧮 Market Capitalization
Market Cap = (Shares Outstanding) × (Share Price)
Shares mostly created at IPO; more can be issued later. A company's own repurchased shares = treasury stock. All shares together = 100% ownership (bonds/options ≠ ownership!).
  • Merger example: A buys B @ 1.2 A-shares per B-share ⟹ A issues new shares and/or uses treasury stock.
  • Dilution: new shares water down existing owners' % (1M shares → +1M more ⟹ originals halved). Existing holders often get antidilution provisions (maintain proportion — at a cost).
  • Shares ≈ fixed; price changes intraday (exchanges publish price + volume). Takeover buyer needs ≈ shares × price + premium ~30%+ (holders sense the ambition) + net debt.
✏️ Share Split 1,000,000 shares @ $40 = $40M cap → 2-for-1 split → 2,000,000 @ $20. Market cap UNCHANGED; stock more affordable (round lot of 100: $2,000 vs $4,000) ⟹ easier trading. Reverse splits happen too.

⭐ Facts Large caps: established, do well in good AND poor economies — Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta; older: Visa, Mastercard, J&J. 2018: Apple = first ever $1 trillion market cap. Safer: experienced management, existing revenue lines, market share. Small caps: usually newer. Apple's 1980 IPO cap: $1.8 billion (mid-cap by that era's thresholds).

2. Growth vs Income Stocks

📘 Growth stock Income → retained earnings to grow competitiveness: better products, new products, geographic expansion, new markets. No dividends ⟹ valuation hard; long-term capital-gain play. Microsoft: IPO 1986, first dividend 2003 — growth stock until 2003, then an income stock. Companies change categories as they evolve!
📘 Income stock Expected to pay dividends; has a historical dividend yield ($200 stock, $10/yr div = 5% yield). Usually quarterly, based on earnings + industry peers. Dividend skip ⟹ swift & potentially extreme reaction — sharp price fall + negative sentiment.

vs bonds: income stocks look "fixed-income-like", but there's no guaranteed amount/date — in bonds, "fixed" = a (default-barring) guarantee.

3. Domestic vs Foreign Stocks

Domestic = company domiciled in the investor's own country (same economy, same currency); foreign = other countries. Why hold foreign stocks? Exposure to other economies/markets/currencies — supply & demand are local: a sector can fall at home and rise abroad.

✏️ FX kicker Foreign stock flat, but the home currency weakens 10% against the foreign one ⟹ converting back looks like +10% return — capital appreciation from foreign exchange, not equity! International PMs pick countries via equity forecasts AND FX-rate models.

4. Private Equity vs Public Equity

📘 Public equity Shares available to anyone on the exchange. Issuing shares publicly = "going public". Zuckerberg: Facebook's IPO day added billions to his wealth; public listing ⟹ net worth is easy to value.
📘 Private equity Shares issued without a public IPO. Advantages: fewer regulatory requirements (investors = high-net-worth individuals/businesses); few investors ⟹ easier management control; later public offering = early investors "ground floor" upside. The Carlyle Group stays private ⟹ no activist/hostile purchases; no quarterly-earnings/buyout pressures.

5. Bitcoin — categories of crypto

"Do you like crypto?" = "Do you like jazz?" — too broad! (The same applies to stocks: blue chips vs penny stocks — priced in pennies, 1000%+ gains possible, 100% loss also possible.)

⭐ Numbers + THE classification Bitcoin ≈ $800 billion ≈ 40% of ~$2 trillion crypto class (as written). #2 = Ethereum. No domestic/foreign classification (borders transcend; 24/7; "the Bitcoin exchange never closes").

Coins = built directly into their own blockchain (Bitcoin)  vs  Tokens = built on top of an existing blockchain (Ethereum tokens).

6. Bitcoin vs Stocks

  • Stocks = intrinsic value (ownership of firms making goods/services). Crypto = currency with implied value through mutual trust.
  • Required reading (Stellinga Box 1.2): money has 3 criteria — Bitcoin does NOT fulfil them (details in reading notes 👇).
  • BTC nearly impossible to counterfeit (creation protocols) — but it can be stolen (digital account access).
  • Greater Fool Theory: you only make money when someone else is convinced it's worth even more and pays a premium. (Kjærland §2 notes 👇)

7. Bitcoin vs Other Alternative Investments

📘 Alternative investments Investments OUTSIDE mainstream equities / fixed income / traditional classes.
  1. Private equity — long holds, build companies without quarterly whims/public-stockholder distraction; for high-net-worth folks who can forgo dividends & absorb capital depreciation.
  2. Real estate — homes, undeveloped land (later module: residential + commercial).
  3. Commodities — agricultural, financial, natural-resource products. CFTC definition: agricultural goods (EXCEPT onions 🧅 — futures banned by law!), physical goods (gold, aluminium), natural resources (energy, oil, electricity), financial instruments (currency, interest rate). Supply/demand easily rattled by extreme weather, geopolitics, overproduction, pandemics ⟹ worldwide price effects.
  4. Art 🎨 — no periodic cash flows; intriguing for finding "value artists" who appreciate over time. (Levenkron notes 👇)

8. Bitcoin vs Other Cryptos + Moats

  • Bitcoin dominates headlines daily. Demographics (CNBC, Robert Frank): most millennial millionaires store wealth in crypto; 83% of millennial millionaires own crypto.
  • BTC trading = stock investing "fast-forwarded" (a month's worth of volatility in a single day); market open >3× stock-market hours; ~20 social-media mentions/minute.
  • Since launch (~15 yrs): >15,000 cryptos created, ~10,000 active. Dogecoin — started as a joke (founder's dog image) → $18 billion market cap!
📘 Competitive Moat 🏰 The water surrounding a medieval castle = a moat. In business: protection that new entrants can't easily breach. Crypto's barrier is just blockchain tech = LOW ⟹ that's why there are 10,000 coins.
✗ No moat — Peloton case First in-home smart-tablet bike + live/recorded classes; pandemic highs (social distancing). Entrants copied: apps (Zwift), bikes (Echelon), combos — lower upfront + subscription costs ⟹ Peloton slashed prices → still lost customers → ceased bike production (as written). The edge couldn't be protected.
✓ Strong moat — Verizon Fiber service = physical towers: land purchase + tech implementation + servicing ⟹ very high barriers (cost, land access, infrastructure).
📌 Bottom line: crypto is hard to trade/model — data volume + market newness + no traditional valuation methods (unlike equities/fixed income) ⟹ statistics are needed → Lesson 3!

Lesson 3 — Measuring Performance

🔑 Keywords: Percent Return • Log Return • Holding Period Return • Price Range • Standard Deviation • Variance • Coefficient of Variation • Sharpe Ratio

1.1 Arithmetic (Percent) Returns

🧮 Basic + Total Return Buy @ P₁ (time t₁), sell @ P₂ (time t₂):
R = P₂ − P₁P₁   (no dividend)  |   R = P₂ − P₁ + DP₁   (dividend D paid in (t₁, t₂])
Dollar total return = P₂ − P₁ + D. Commissions/fees technically included, but small ⟹ ignore. No dividend ⟹ "return" = "total return".

1.2 Logarithmic Returns

🧮 Log Return
R̃ = ln(P₂ / P₁)   •   with dividend: R̃ = ln((P₂ + D)/P₁)
Tilde (~) distinguishes log from arithmetic. Both are similar but NOT the same.
✏️ Solved Example Bought 2 Jan 2024 @ $125; 28 Feb 2024 worth $153.
Arithmetic: (153−125)/125 = 22.4%   vs   Log: ln(153/125) = ln(1.224) ≈ 20.21%

1.3 Log or Arithmetic? (Derivation — an exam favourite!)

Invest V₀; arithmetic returns R₁,…,R_T over subperiods.

🧮 Continuous compounding (gains reinvested)
V_T = V₀(1+R₁)(1+R₂)···(1+R_T)  ⟹  1 + R₀,T = Π(1+Rₜ)
Take log of both sides:
ln(1+R₀,T) = Σₜ ln(1+Rₜ)   and   ln(1+Rₜ) = ln(Pₜ/Pₜ₋₁) = R̃ₜ
⭐ R̃₀,T = R̃₁ + R̃₂ + ··· + R̃_T — total log return = SUM of subperiod log returns!
⭐ Why log returns win ① ADDITIVITY (property above)  ② Prices log-normal ⟹ returns normal — key for quant models (Derivative Pricing course).  ③ Small returns: ln(1+x) ≈ x ⟹ log ≈ arithmetic.
📘 Rebalancing version At the start of every subperiod, reset back to V₀ (withdraw profit / top up loss):
R*₀,T = R₁ + R₂ + ··· + R_T   (simple sum, no compounding)
A different symbol R* because the computation is different.

2. Time-Specific Returns

  • Daily closes give: daily, weekly (Fri→Fri; any weekday works), monthly (last trading day), quarterly (Mar 31/Jun 30/Sep 30/Dec 31), annual (Dec 31 → Dec 31).
  • ⚠️ You can lengthen, not shorten — you can't get weekly/daily returns from monthly data.
  • Weekends/holidays: providers fill Sat/Sun with Friday's price ⟹ daily data's volatility can look artificially low (less of an issue for weekly/monthly). Crypto trades 24/7 ⟹ no such gap — keep this data-availability difference in mind when mixing stocks and crypto!
  • n prices ⟹ n−1 daily returns (day 1's return needs one more price before it). Weekly from n daily prices ⟹ ~n/5 returns. Models need lots of data points; yearly returns mean very long histories (over which the underlying models may have already changed).

Annualizing ⏱️

✏️ Example $100,000 (1 Jul) → $110,000 (31 Dec): 6-month return 10%. 12/6 = 2 periods/yr ⟹ annualized ≈ 20% (simple extrapolation).
⚠️ The trap of annualization $2 → $2.20 in ONE day = 10%/day ⟹ "3650% annualized — Warren Buffett who?" 🤡 Absurd: it won't repeat; tomorrow it could just as easily be $1.95. Actual annual return (points truly 1 yr apart) ≠ annualized (extrapolated).

GIPS (Global Investment Performance Standards): presenting extrapolated annualized returns is FORBIDDEN — show a partial year as a partial year. Also watch out for funds that only report their best years!
✏️ De-annualization Firm starts 1 Nov 2023; 2-month return 4% ⟹ annualized 26.53% (compound). If a non-GIPS firm advertises 26.53%, a savvy investor checks:
(1 + 0.2653)^(2/12) − 1 = 1.2653^(1/6) − 1 ≈ 4% ✓ actual 2-month performance
📘 Holding Period Return Total gain over the whole hold, NOT annualized; time is ignored. $100,000 → $170,000 ⟹ HPR = 70% (whether over 7 days or 7 years). It just tells you whether there was a profit or not.
📘 Net Return ALL incomes & costs included:
Net R = Inflows − OutflowsOutflows
✏️ Net return example Buy $100 + $2 commission; dividend $7; sell $115:
Net R = (115 + 7 − 102)/102 = 20/102 ≈ 19.61%
⚠️ Commission = OUTFLOW (cost of entering). Taxes ignored (global audience, rates vary) — but they only LOWER actual returns.

3–4. From Returns to Volatility

Expected return = an average (the expected value of a random distribution). We also need the expected distance from the average ⟹ standard deviation = central volatility measure. (Lottery: negative EV but tiny loss vs huge improbable win — an exception.)

Markets = complex systems: many participants, economies, models, risk tolerances, cash needs, taxes (LT vs ST capital gains taxed differently). News drives volatility: it arrives unevenly, importance varies, absorption differs — algorithms sell instantly on headline sentiment, professionals react slower, others pause & buy ⟹ volatility changes.

5. Volatility Measures

📘 (a) Price Range — units of PRICE
Range = Day's High − Day's Low
Friday: high 105, low 99 ⟹ $6; Monday: 106/104 ⟹ $2 ⟹ Friday more volatile. Needs intraday high/low ("high/low-water marks"); closing prices insufficient. Perfectly lucky day trader (buy low, sell high) earns exactly the range.
📘 (b) SD of returns — units of RETURN SD of the last 20 daily returns (⟹ 21 trading days ≈ 1 NYSE month). SD = expected distance from the average.
Daily mean 0.2%, SD 0.5%, returns ~Normal ⟹ 68% of returns ∈ (0.2−0.5, 0.2+0.5) = (−0.3%, +0.7%).
⚠️ (c) Variance = SD² — units of SQUARED returns Do NOT use variance and SD interchangeably! Deviations are ±; for volatility we need magnitude, not sign ⟹ square them (or take absolute value). Variance = average squared deviation; SD = √variance (units back to correct). 20% SD ⟹ variance 4%. In everyday conversation, "volatility" usually = SD.

6. Coefficient of Variation (CV)

🧮 CV = volatility ÷ return
CV = σ / R   →   "how much volatility you have to spend per unit of return" (like an exchange rate)
Stock A: 50 vol-units/return-unit; Stock B: 25 ⟹ B is the bargain — LOWER CV better. Caveat: this assumes good representative samples and a correct calculation.

7. Sharpe Ratio

🧮 Sharpe = return ÷ volatility (market premium version)
Sharpe = R − R_fσ    (numerator = market premium)
R_f = risk-free rate: most conservative asset (risk-free bond) ka return. Stocks should earn MORE than R_f — otherwise a rational investor would just stay in the risk-free asset.
✏️ Example Stock C: premium 7%, σ 10% ⟹ 0.70  |  Stock D: premium 8%, σ 12% ⟹ 0.667 ⟹ prefer C (more return per unit risk). Works across asset classes.
⭐ Direction rule (memorize!) CV (vol per return) → LOWER better   vs   Sharpe (return per vol) → HIGHER better

Lesson 4 — Modeling Performance

🔑 Keywords: Normal (Gaussian) Distribution • Skewness • Kurtosis • Student's t • Lognormal

1. Prices over Time

  • Time-series plot: price (y) vs time (x). Stock prices NEVER go below zero; bond yields can go negative.
  • You can't read return directly off a price chart (it's entry/exit dependent); volatility can be eyeballed (range). (Text figure: Netflix COVID-19 rally.)
  • Chart questions: how high/low? oscillation frequency? trend? volatility clustering? does it rise more abruptly than it falls?
⚠️ Classic exam Q&A Q: Why CAN'T stock prices be normally distributed?
A: The normal distribution allows negative values — prices can't go negative! ✔️ If prices are log-normal ⟹ returns are normal (consistent with Lesson 3).

2. Distribution of Returns

  • Prices → daily returns: y-axis return units, center ≈ 0, signs alternate. Different from a cumulative return chart (which tracks trades from a fixed start).
  • Time scale ↔ purpose: intraday/daily = financial properties/trading detail (but idiosyncratic-news noise); monthly/quarterly = economic cycles (but trading opportunities missed). Always collect the highest frequency — aggregating up is always possible, disaggregating down never is (Econometrics: some properties only show up in daily data).
  • Drop the time order and collapse the returns ⟹ analyze them as a sample/distribution — this lesson's focus.
⭐ Normal distribution = "powerful zip file" 🗜️ Billions of points → 2 parameters (mean + SD) — like voters electing 2 representatives! These let you easily estimate percentiles/probabilities ⟹ trading strategies, portfolio optimization, risk management.
⚠️ But we can't just assume it — CLT fails here Central Limit Theorem: i.i.d. random variables ka sum → Gaussian. Is Monday's stock behaviour i.i.d. with Tuesday's? NO — participants adapt/change behaviour ⟹ trading days neither independent nor identically distributed ⟹ TEST for normality, don't assume it.

3. Symmetry & Skewness

  • Normal ⟹ symmetric by construction (standard normal: mean 0, SD 1; place a mirror on the y-axis — perfect match).
  • Negatively skewed = longer LEFT tail (large losses possible)  |  positively skewed = longer RIGHT tail.
  • Practical danger: negatively-skewed returns + normal assumption = grossly underestimated loss potential (left tail ignored). Equity markets' common pattern: panic days with large drops, without matching large up-days. (Behavioral finance: humans remember painful events more.)
  • Finance ≠ physics — more like evolutionary biology + cognitive biases + socially influenced decisions.

Visual checks: ① histogram (are the bars equal on both sides?) ② density plot ("mound of sand" per point — is the shape normal-like?).

🧮 Skewness formulas
Pearson's 1st coefficient: Skew = Mean − Modeσ   (symmetric ⟹ mean = median ⟹ skew = 0)
Sample: Skew ≈ (1/n) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)³ / s³
(The course won't lean heavily on the math — remember the CONCEPTS: symmetric / +ve / −ve skew + risk implications.)
⭐ Rule Symmetric distribution ⟹ SD acceptable risk measure. Skewed ⟹ generally NOT.

4. Constant Volatility? — Mixture of Normals

✏️ The mixture experiment Mix: 80% × N(0, 1) + 20% × N(0, 4) ⟹ mixture SD ≈ 2. Reliable? Check excess kurtosis = quality check on the variance ("variance of the variance"). Pure normal: variance known with certainty ⟹ excess kurtosis 0. Each component alone: ≈ 0. Mixture: excess kurtosis ≈ 7 (≈ 10 in the text's other example) ⟹ NON-Gaussian!
Later in the program: "vol of vol" (in option pricing).
⚠️ Danger A wrong SD ⟹ wrong percentiles + a false sense of security. The art of financial engineering = knowing where models work & where they break down.

5. Normal coverage rules (memorize!)

🧮 Gaussian coverage
±1σ → 68%   •   ±2σ → 95.5%   •   ±3σ → 99.7%
Excess kurtosis significantly > 0 ⟹ normality likely violated ⟹ SD is no longer a reliable expected-distance measure.

6. Normal or Not?

  • Distributions that allow skewness: F, skew-normal, log-normal + others (Financial Data course). Formal tests exist; robust measures: ranks, or one-sided returns (semi-variance — only negative returns).
  • Kurtosis: peaked vs flat? Peaked ⟹ lighter tails; flat ⟹ heavier tails (relevant for risk).
🧮 Kurtosis
Kurt ≈ (1/n) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)⁴ / s⁴   •   Standard normal kurtosis ≈ 3  ⟹  Excess kurtosis = Kurt − 3
Excess > 0 ⟹ heavier tails than normal  |  Excess < 0 ⟹ thinner tails.
📘 Student's t-distribution Symmetric (asymmetric versions exist); HAS excess kurtosis; described by ONE parameter — degrees of freedom (df). Small df (e.g., 4) ⟹ high excess kurtosis; df → ∞ ⟹ t → normal.
⭐ Decision framework If skew or excess kurtosis is present ⟹ normal is discredited. Returns normal ⟹ life is easy (mean + SD suffice). Not normal ⟹ ① switch to a skew/kurtosis-reflecting distribution (needs more modeling skill) or ② go nonparametric (raw data, no distribution). Tool: a Shiny app — select high-volatility date ranges and watch the distribution change.

Volatility takeaway: volatility = the fluctuation investors tolerate; the preference is investor-specific. The job of Financial Engineering: metrics/models that represent volatility even when it's dynamic. Next lesson: correlation (pairs of securities).

📖 Reading 1 — Stellinga et al., Money & Debt: The Public Role of Banks (Springer 2021), Ch. 1

Authors: Bart Stellinga, Josta de Hoog, Arthur van Riel, Casper de Vries (Netherlands Scientific Council for Government Policy — WRR) • Names + numbers preserved for assignment citations!
🧭 Context Translation of a Dutch WRR report, at the request of the Dutch Finance Minister — citizens' initiative Ons Geld ("Our Money") gathered 120,000+ signatures for public money creation. Post-2007–09-crisis reform debates: Positive Money (UK), Martin Wolf (FT), IMF/Bank of England research, Icelandic parliament proposal, Swiss Vollgeld referendum (June 2018): 24% for, 76% against.
⭐ Core surprising fact Most money is created NOT by governments/central banks but by COMMERCIAL BANKS — money created when banks grant loans, destroyed when loans repaid ⟹ money & debt = two sides of the same coin; money-supply growth inseparable from debt growth. Pre-crisis economists treated money/debt as an insignificant "veil" over real activity.

1.1 What is money?

  • Money = an (implicit) agreement between people — a SOCIAL CONSTRUCT. Asset serves as money only when people are confident others will accept it; value rests on confidence, not material. Historical forms: coins, shells, salt, coral, banknotes, digital ones-and-zeros.
  • Trust must be generated & maintained: historically precious-metal verification + anti-counterfeiting law; today for deposits: deposit guarantee schemes, banking supervision, central-bank liquidity support.
  • 3 origin theories: ① response to barter inefficiencies (textbook story — NOT backed by historical evidence; Menger: "no one invented money") ② origins in debt relationships (promissory notes circulating detached from issuer/recipient) ③ role of state/religious authorities (money from taxes/offerings).
🧮 THE THREE FUNCTIONS OF MONEY (the most important part!)
  1. Means of payment — generally accepted purchasing power (goods/services, wages, debts, shares, tax). Must be transferable without losing utility, divisible, and sufficiently stable in value.
  2. Store of value (means of saving) — retains value over time; not easily debased. Unlike shares/bonds/jewellery/real estate, money is highly LIQUID (instantly usable as payment).
  3. Unit of account — compare unlike things (bread vs iPhone; countries' GDP).
The report's working definition: money = what is generally accepted as a means of payment ⟹ today mainly cash + instantly available bank deposits (+ quasi-money).
📘 Monetary aggregates M1 = cash + demand deposits (narrowest)  •  M2, M3 = wider (add savings, money-market fund shares…). M0 = cash + central-bank reserves (base money).
M3 composition (Sept 2018, NL): 7% cash • 39% payment-account deposits • 51% savings deposits (<2 yr maturity / <3 mo notice) • 3% other. Boundaries of "money" fluid — innovation creates money-like products, e.g., cryptocurrencies.
⚠️⭐ Box 1.2 — Is Bitcoin Money? (THE LESSON EXPLICITLY POINTS HERE — READ IT) Cryptos = digital currencies generated by cryptography. Bitcoin launched October 2008, just after the fall of Lehman Brothers. Developers' hope: alternative to money dependent on governments/central banks/commercial banks — trust instead on "objective, rational, incorruptible mathematical calculations" in a decentralized, shared, publicly accessible accounting system.

VERDICT: Bitcoin does NOT fulfil money's three functions:
  1. Means of payment ✗ — ability to pay with BTC extremely limited;
  2. Store of value ✗ — too volatile (>$20,000 Dec 2017 → ~$6,000 summer 2018) — unsuitable for saving, though fine for speculation;
  3. Unit of account ✗ — handful of products priced in BTC; users need a real-time app for dollar/euro worth.
The box's lesson: building a stable currency is HARD. Limited supply + decentralized verification ≠ guaranteed stability; mathematical trust-generation has clear limits. Euro/dollar backed by institutions whose explicit purpose is guarantees & stability; open question: can a crypto without institutional backing ever be an alternative?

1.2 What is debt?

  • Debt framed economically AND morally (Dutch/German schuld = debt + guilt; Bible: "Let love be your only debt"; Islam prohibits interest; centuries of European hostility). Yet debt-financed investment crucial: Ferguson — credit/debt rival all technological innovations in fuelling civilization (ice-maker, farmer's fertilizer, education loan — using future income today).
  • Graeber: debt = "an exchange that has not been brought to completion" (non-simultaneous exchange) ⟹ time gap ⟹ repayment risk ⟹ requires MUTUAL TRUST (hence "creditor"; German Gläubiger = believer).
  • Keynes: "Owe your banker £1,000 and you are at his mercy; owe him £1 million and the position is reversed."
  • Not all debt voluntary (tax debt); unequal parties ⟹ usury laws, max interest rates. Repayment limits: debtors' prisons gone (Dickens' Marshalsea); Netherlands first in Europe to decriminalize bankruptcy — Amsterdam Desolate Boedelkamer (1627) — which is why Rembrandt was able to paint again! Lender accepts some risk.
📘 Box 1.3 — Is money always debt? Current system: money created with loans; bank deposits ARE bank debt to account holders. BUT — not all money is debt: CASH is NOT debt (no gold or anything else is exchanged with the central bank; "debt" recording is somewhat arbitrary vs equity). Debt can serve as money if divisible & transferable; not all debt is money, not all money is debt. Money is always a claim on current/future production ⟹ unlimited creation risks inflation.

1.3 Trust

  • Trust crucial for money & lending; the crisis shook it. Ons Geld + crypto popularity = expressions of MISTRUST in the current system.
  • Dutch data: banks have the least trust among institutions; strikingly, highly educated people trust the banking system LESS (the OPPOSITE of the usual pattern!). DNB survey: economists trust financial-system stability LESS than the general public; trust correlated with belief that regulation sufficiently tightened.
  • Trust cannot be enforced — earned through proven reliability + ability to voice dissatisfaction. Report gauges: economic contribution, stability, fairness (reliability) + influence, legitimacy (dissatisfaction).

1.4 Dynamism of the system

  • System = banks, central bank, public authorities, pension funds, insurers, shadow banks (non-bank institutions doing bank-like lending on short-term debt, but cannot create deposit money). Constantly changing: industrialization, globalization, tech (payments in milliseconds).
  • New tech → cryptos + innovations; central banks study CBDC (central bank digital currency). Haldane: money "is a social convention", not just cryptographic code.
  • Box 1.4 Fintech: Lagarde's definition — new technologies affecting financial services: AI, big data, biometrics, distributed ledger technologies (blockchain). Functions: payments, savings, finance, insurance (+ IMF's 5th: advice). Small fintechs don't yet threaten big banks (licences, size, expertise, guarantees); real question = bigtech (Amazon Cash, Google Pay; later Facebook's Libra). Regulation-dependent ⟹ continuous political oversight essential.
  • Other uncertainties: record debt/GDP; near-zero ECB rates; massive bond purchases; negative-interest bonds (governments/companies effectively RECEIVE interest from lenders!).
🎯 Ch.1 exam nuggets: commercial banks create most money via lending • 3 functions of money • Bitcoin fails all three (Box 1.2) • money = social construct on trust • debt = incomplete/non-simultaneous exchange (Graeber) • cash is money but NOT debt.

📖 Reading 2 — Kjærland et al., "An Analysis of Bitcoin's Price Dynamics" (JRFM 2018)

Authors: Frode Kjærland, Aras Khazal, Erlend A. Krogstad, Frans B. G. Nordstrøm, Are Oust • J. Risk & Financial Mgmt 11(4):63 • Required: Section 2 (pp. 2–3)
🧭 Paper's aim Identify Bitcoin's price drivers (weekly data 2013–2018, incl. volatile late-2017/early-2018) using ARDL (Autoregressive Distributed Lag) + GARCH models — help investors make sound decisions. BTC: $0.07 (2009) → all-time high $20,089 on 17 Dec 2017; 2017 saw +1270%, "Bitcoin's IPO moment".

§2.1 — Intro to cryptocurrencies & Bitcoin

  • Bitcoin's major innovation = decentralized technology (Dwyer 2015): database (blockchain) distributed across participating computers, not one server.
  • Mining = solving complex computational puzzles; incentives = transaction fees + Bitcoin rewards.
  • Hashrate = speed at which a computer completes an operation in the Bitcoin code; mining difficulty = puzzle complexity, directly correlated with hashrate. Hashrate rise/fall ⟹ algorithm adjusts difficulty so supply follows a predetermined path; new coins ≈ every 10 minutes, price-independent.
⭐ KEY CONCEPT — Deterministic supply Bitcoin supply is DETERMINISTIC — inelastic, dependent SOLELY on time, NOT on price (unlike a normal good). This is the foundation of the paper.

§2.3 — Theoretical foundation ⭐ (this is exactly why the lesson points here!)

📘 Efficient Market Hypothesis (Fama 1976; via Santoni 1987) ALL relevant info is in current prices; prices change only on new fundamental information ⟹ past price changes contain NO useful info about future changes.
🃏 Greater Fool Theory (Santoni 1987) Investors treat fundamentals as IRRELEVANT; buy believing a "bigger fool" will pay more later — pure speculation, anticipating rises only because prices rose before.
📘 Momentum Theory (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993, 2001) Empirically: rising prices rise further, falling prices fall further; strong past performers outperform weak past performers. Relies on short-term movements, not fundamentals; cause = cognitive bias / irrational investor behaviour.
📘 Volatility & safe havens Turmoil ⟹ investors shift to safe-haven assets (currency = safe-haven if demand RISES in financial stress). Glosten et al. (1993): negative equity shocks generate more volatility than positive. Baur & McDermott (2012): gold's volatility reacts INVERSELY to negative shocks (safe-haven property; rising gold price = rising macro uncertainty). Bitcoin: Bouri et al. (2016) — no asymmetric return-volatility relation (supports safe-haven); Kjærland et al. (2018) — opposite finding.

Key findings of the paper (assignment gold 🥇)

⭐ Finding #1 — Hashrate is IRRELEVANT Supply is deterministic ⟹ adding processing power does NOT increase output ⟹ putting hashrate into a price model is wrong. Causality runs the OTHER way: price → hashrate (price↑ → mining becomes profitable → more miners/power until excess profit = 0; price drop → power pulled out). This CONTRADICTS previous literature (Bouoiyour & Selmi, Garcia, Georgoula, Hayes, Kristoufek, Sovbetov…) — the paper's primary contribution.
✓ Significant drivers
  • S&P 500 — largest coefficient (ARDL: S&P +1% ⟹ BTC +1.41–1.77%). Market optimism spills into Bitcoin ⟹ BTC investors likely risk-seeking. (Supported by Yermack 2013, Dyhrberg 2016.)
  • Google searches — attention ⟹ demand. Short-run effect ~0.11–0.30%, long-run ~0.37%. (Consistent with Kristoufek, Ciaian et al.)
  • Last week's BTC return (lag ~0.19%) ⟹ EMH seemingly does NOT hold; consistent with Momentum + Greater Fool behaviour (confusing context, too much new info, quick decisions, get-rich-quick psychology).
✗ Insignificant VIX (except 2017–18 sub-period: NEGATIVE relation ⟹ Bitcoin does NOT show safe-haven properties — inconsistent with Bouri et al. 2017a/b), oil, gold (⟹ BTC lacks commodity properties; in line with Bouri et al. 2018b), transaction volume (in ARDL; significant negative in GARCH). GARCH: ARCH+GARCH effects ≈ 87.7–90.5% ⟹ strong volatility persistence.

  5. CLT fails for markets: trading days NOT i.i.d. ⟹ test for normality, don't assume it.
