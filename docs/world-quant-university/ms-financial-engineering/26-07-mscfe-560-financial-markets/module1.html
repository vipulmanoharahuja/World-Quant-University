LESSON 1

SAVING AND BORROWING

🔑 Keywords: Time Value of Money, Interest Rate, Principal, Maturity, Spot Rate, Forward Rate, Simple & Compound Interest, Yield Curve

1. Time Value of Money (TVM)

₹1 / $1 today is worth MORE than ₹1 / $1 in the future.

Why? Two reasons:

  • Investment reason: If I get $1,000 today, I can invest it at 5% → after 1 year I have $1,050. So $1,000 today = $1,050 next year. I am indifferent between the two.
  • Human nature reason: People love spending NOW (instant gratification). I will only delay my spending (i.e., save) if I am rewarded with extra money later.

2. Interest Rates

Interest rate = cost of the loan (for borrower) = compensation for waiting + taking risk (for lender).

Lender decides the rate based on:

  • Purpose of the loan
  • Market / economic conditions (recession → repayment uncertain)
  • Financial reputation of the borrower
  • Amount & duration of the loan
Longer duration + Bigger amount = Riskier loan = Higher interest rate. Also banks compete with each other, so competition can lower rates.

3. Simple vs Compound Interest

Simple Interest

Interest is earned only on the principal, every year, flat.

FV = P (1 + r·t) P = principal, r = annual rate, t = time in years

Compound Interest

Interest is earned on principal + previously earned interest → "Interest earns interest!"

FV = P (1 + r)t
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅

Q. Deposit $100,000 for 10 years. Bank A: 6% simple. Bank B: 5% compound. Which bank?

  1. Bank A (simple): FV = 100,000 (1 + 0.06 × 10) = 100,000 × 1.6 = $160,000
  2. Bank B (compound): FV = 100,000 (1.05)10 = 100,000 × 1.62889 = $162,889.46
  3. Answer: Bank B wins! Even with a LOWER rate, compounding grows faster over long time.

👉 As a saver → prefer compound. As a borrower → prefer simple!

Fig 1: Compound interest overtakes simple interest over time 🚀

4. Present Value (PV) & Forward/Future Value (FV)

FV = PV (1 + r)t   ⟺   PV = FV / (1 + r)t   ⟺   r = (FV/PV)1/t − 1
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅

Q. We lend $1,000 and receive $1,250 after 2 years. What is our return r?

  1. FV = 1250, PV = 1000, t = 2
  2. r = (1250/1000)1/2 − 1 = (1.25)0.5 − 1 = 1.1180 − 1
  3. r ≈ 0.1180 = 11.80% per year
From now on, unless stated otherwise, assume COMPOUND interest in all questions. (Simple interest is used in special cases like some CDs.)

5. Converting Compounding Frequencies

Nominal rate r₁ compounded n₁ times/year ⟷ equivalent rate r₂ compounded n₂ times/year. The effective (final) money must be equal for both.

(1 + r₁/n₁)n₁ = (1 + r₂/n₂)n₂
DERIVATION 🔍
Take 1 year. Compounding n₁ times gives: FV₁ = P(1 + r₁/n₁)n₁
Compounding n₂ times gives: FV₂ = P(1 + r₂/n₂)n₂
➜ For fairness (no one should gain/lose): FV₁ = FV₂
➜ (1 + r₂/n₂)n₂ = (1 + r₁/n₁)n₁
➜ Raise both sides to power 1/n₂: 1 + r₂/n₂ = (1 + r₁/n₁)n₁/n₂
➜ r₂ = n₂[ (1 + r₁/n₁)n₁/n₂ − 1 ] ✔
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅

Q. Bank pays 5% compounded monthly (n₁=12). What is the equivalent rate with quarterly compounding (n₂=4)?

  1. r₂ = 4[ (1 + 0.05/12)12/4 − 1 ] = 4[ (1.0041667)3 − 1 ]
  2. (1.0041667)³ = 1.012552 → r₂ = 4 × 0.012552
  3. r₂ ≈ 0.050209 = 5.021%
  4. Check with $10,000: Monthly: 10,000(1+0.05/12)¹² ≈ $10,511.62. Quarterly: 10,000(1+0.050209/4)⁴ ≈ $10,511.62 ✔ Same!

6. Continuous Compounding

Compound yearly → monthly → daily → every second → infinite frequency (m → ∞)

DERIVATION 🔍
FV = P(1 + r/m)mt ... compounding m times a year for t years
Take natural log: ln(FV) = ln(P) + mt·ln(1 + r/m)
➜ Key limit: as m → ∞, m·ln(1 + r/m) → r   (since ln(1+x) ≈ x for small x)
➜ So ln(FV) = ln(P) + rt  ⟹  FV = P·ert ✔
FV = P · ert (continuous compounding)
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅ (Doubling time)

Q. $10,000 invested at 6% continuously compounded. How many years to DOUBLE?

  1. 10,000 · e0.06t = 20,000 → e0.06t = 2
  2. Take ln both sides: 0.06t = ln 2 = 0.6931
  3. t = 0.6931 / 0.06 ≈ 11.55 years
  4. ⭐ Answer does NOT depend on the amount invested!

💬 Einstein (reportedly): "Compound interest is the 8th wonder of the world. He who understands it, earns it… he who doesn't, pays it."

7. Yield Curve

Yield curve = graph of interest rates vs maturity for Treasury bonds.

  • Normal curve (upward slope): long-term rates > short-term rates (long-term = riskier → investors demand more).
  • Inverted curve: short-term rates > long-term rates → market thinks near-term is risky → often a warning of RECESSION (govt will cut rates to stimulate economy).
Fig 2: Normal vs Inverted Yield Curve

8. Spot Rates & Forward Rates

  • Spot rate rt: the rate available TODAY for an investment starting now and maturing at time t.
  • Forward rate f(0; t₁, t₂): the rate (decided today, at time 0) for an investment that starts at t₁ and ends at t₂.
LOGIC / DERIVATION 🔍 (No-Arbitrage)
Route 1: Invest directly for t₂ years → grow by (1 + rt₂)t₂
Route 2: Invest for t₁ years, then reinvest at forward rate f for (t₂ − t₁) years → grow by (1 + rt₁)t₁(1 + f)t₂−t₁
➜ Both routes must give the SAME result (else free profit = arbitrage):
(1 + rt₂)t₂ = (1 + rt₁)t₁ · (1 + f)t₂−t₁ ⟹ f = [ (1+rt₂)t₂ / (1+rt₁)t₁ ]1/(t₂−t₁) − 1
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅

Given spot rates: 1-yr = 4%, 2-yr = 4.5%, 3-yr = 5%. Find f(0;1,2) — the 1-year rate starting after year 1.

  1. (1.045)² = (1.04)¹ (1+f)¹
  2. 1.092025 = 1.04 (1+f) → 1+f = 1.050024
  3. f(0;1,2) ≈ 5.0024% ≈ 5%

Similarly f(0;1,3) (2-year rate starting at year 1): (1.05)³ = (1.04)(1+f)² → (1+f)² = 1.157625/1.04 = 1.113101 → 1+f = 1.055036 → f ≈ 5.50%

9. Variable Interest Rates (Flex CD)

Rate is linked to a benchmark, e.g., Fed funds rate − 0.3%. When benchmark moves, our rate moves:

  • Fed rate 3% → CD earns 2.7% ; Fed rises to 3.5% → CD earns 3.2% ; Fed 4% → CD earns 3.7% …
  • Future interest is uncertain — cannot calculate exactly in advance.
Expect rates to RISE → choose variable (as saver). Expect rates to FALL → lock a fixed rate. For a mortgage (borrower) the logic is OPPOSITE — you want to pay less!

10. Nominal vs Real Rates — Fisher Equation

  • Nominal rate (n): the rate you actually earn on paper.
  • Inflation (i): rise in prices → your money loses purchasing power. "Inflation is a thief!"
  • Real rate (r): your TRUE gain in purchasing power.
Approx (Fisher): real ≈ nominal − inflation
Exact: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real)(1 + inflation)
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅

Nominal = 3%, Inflation = 2% → real ≈ 3 − 2 = 1% (barely beat inflation).
If inflation = 5% → real ≈ 3 − 5 = −2% (NEGATIVE!) → you LOST purchasing power. Better to spend today! 🍟

Fisher Effect: When expected inflation rises, investors demand higher nominal rates — because they care about real returns. (Approx formula fails when n or i are large — use exact one.)

11. Saving Rate vs Borrowing Rate

Same bank: pays you 3% on deposits but charges 7.5% on loans (credit card 18%+ 😱).

Why the gap? Deposits are the bank's raw material; loans are its product. Product price > raw material cost = bank's profit. Banks borrow short (deposits) & lend long (mortgages).

MINI SUM ✅ (simple interest, 1 yr, $10,000)

As depositor you earn: 10,000 × 3% = $300. As borrower you pay: 10,000 × 7.5% = $750.

12. Term to Maturity

Term to maturity = how long the money stays locked (start today → future date when principal returns). CDs have a fixed term; normal savings accounts have no term (withdraw anytime) but pay very low rates.

Rule: Longer maturity → higher rate. Why? More time = more uncertainty (inflation, economy, your own needs) → you deserve extra reward for the longer lock-up.

13. Default (intro)

Longer loans also mean more time for the borrower's situation to worsen (job loss, economy). If borrower fails to pay = DEFAULT → detailed in Lesson 2.

14. Applied TVM — Retirement Problems ⭐

PROBLEM A — FULL SOLUTION ✅ (very important!)

Q. Robert (25 yrs, hired 2024) wants $1,000,000 in today's money at end of 2064. Discount rate 3%. He deposits $H at each year-end from 2024–2063 (40 deposits, none in 2064). Investments earn 5%. Find H.

  1. Step 1 — Convert target to future money: $1M today = 1,000,000 × (1.03)40 = 1,000,000 × 3.26204 = $3,262,037.79 needed in 2064.
  2. Step 2 — Value of 40 deposits at 5%: The deposits form an annuity. Their value at the end is:
    H(1.05)40 + H(1.05)39 + … + H(1.05)¹ … (first deposit compounds 40 yrs, last one 1 yr as he retires end-2064)
  3. Step 3 — Geometric series: Sum = H × 1.05 × [(1.05)40 − 1]/0.05
  4. Step 4 — Solve: H × 126.8398 = 3,262,037.79 → H ≈ $25,717.79 per year ✔
PROBLEM B type (spend $150,000/yr for 20 yrs after retiring): same logic but two annuities — accumulation phase & withdrawal phase. Best solved in a spreadsheet — set FV of deposits = PV of withdrawals at retirement date.
LESSON 2

COUNTERPARTIES & CREDIT RISK

🔑 Keywords: Credit Risk, Collateral, Regulation, Deposit Insurance, Bonds

1. Risks in Finance

Finance is full of risk — "no pain, no gain". A quant's job = recognize, measure, hedge risks.

Risk = SYSTEMATIC (hits whole market) + NON-SYSTEMATIC (asset-specific)
RiskMeaning (simple)Example
EconomicBad economy hurts businesses/countriesRecession hits a new business
EquityStock prices fall unfavourablyShares drop after buying
Interest rateRates move against youBank gave 5% mortgages; rates jump to 8% → bank sad 😢. Or your 4.5% coupon bond vs new 6.4% bonds → sell below par → capital loss
ModelWrong model → wrong answersBad pricing model → bad investments

2. Overdue Payment vs Default

  • Overdue payment: payment past the due date but usually gets paid — late fees, grace periods apply. (Sometimes just holidays/weekends delay processing.)
  • Default: an overdue payment that remains UNPAID — borrower fails on interest, principal, or both. Much more serious!

Debt covenants: written restrictions lenders impose on companies (in the loan agreement) so companies don't over-borrow. Default on ONE loan can trigger default on ALL loans → if no liquidity → bankruptcy.

📖 Fun fact: "Bankruptcy" comes from Italian banca rotta = "broken bench" — money dealers who ran out of money had their bench broken → out of business! Today, bankruptcy laws protect borrowers (no debtor's prison), but their credit reputation suffers → future loans cost more.

3. Credit Risk

Credit Risk = risk that the LENDER does not receive full principal + interest promised in the contract
  • Caused by inability or even unwillingness of the borrower to pay.
  • Affects the lender, not the borrower.
  • Needs a contractual obligation — don't confuse with market risk!
CONCEPT CHECK ✅

Q. Investor buys 10,000 shares @ $15; price falls to $12 and company cancels dividends. Student says "this is credit risk". Correct?

A. NO! Dividends are at the company's discretion (not promised like bond coupons), and the company never guaranteed share value. This is market/equity risk, NOT credit risk. Credit risk needs a contractual promise.

4. Deposit Insurance 🛡

Banks don't keep your money in a locker — they invest/lend it. Historically, bad investments → bank runs → depositors lost money.

Solution: Deposit insurance corporations (members of IADI internationally):

  • USA → FDIC: insured up to $250,000 per depositor
  • South Korea → KDIC: up to ₩20 million per depositor (₩50M per insurance policy holder)

They guarantee deposits (with govt backing) even if the bank fails → builds trust & confidence in the entire financial system. Banks = cornerstone of finance.

SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅

Q. You have $100,000 in a US bank. Bank fails, says it will pay only 40 cents per dollar. How much do you get?

A. If FDIC insured → full $100,000 back (limit is $250,000, you're within it ✔). If NOT insured → 100,000 × 0.40 = $40,000 only 😔

5. Collateral

Collateral = financial or physical asset kept as backup for the loan.

  • Car loan → the car is collateral. Pay on time → keep using the car. Default → bank takes the car.
  • Banks don't actually want cars (they're not in the auto business!) — collateral just protects them so they can focus on banking.

6. Types of Financial Institutions

"Too big to fail": if a giant institution collapses, it can wreck the whole system (deposits gone, pensions unpaid, panic). Answer = REGULATION.

7. Regulation ⚖

Football analogy ⚽: rules + referees make the game safe, fair & trustworthy. Without rules → fouls, cheating, chaos. Same in finance:

  • Regulation = rules + enforcement (fines, penalties for breaking them).
  • Protects ALL stakeholders: consumers, taxpayers, workers, pensioners, investors, businesses… everyone.
  • Post-crisis example: no-documentation loans became much harder → banks must verify borrowers properly → lower aggregate credit risk.
  • Hard part: with billions of transactions, finding the culprit / whose authority applies is tough.

8. Basics of BONDS 📜

Bond = debt security: issuer (borrower) pays bondholder (lender) periodic INTEREST (coupons) + repays PRINCIPAL (face value) at maturity
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅ (US Treasury bond)

Issued 1/1/2024, maturity 12/31/2025, coupon 4%, notional $20 billion, semi-annual payments.

  1. Each coupon = 20,000,000,000 × 4%/2 = $400,000,000 every 6 months
  2. Dates: 6/30/24, 12/31/24, 6/30/25, 12/31/25
  3. Principal repayment: $20B on 12/31/2025

⭐ Rule: semi-annual coupon = (coupon rate ÷ 2) × face value

Why issue bonds? Govt → fund public projects (roads, bridges, schools). Companies → finance projects at a cost lower than the project's return.

Bond vs CD (both are "fixed income")

3 ways to get paid from a bond: ① wait for coupon dates ② hold till maturity → principal back ③ sell before maturity at market price.

LESSON 3

BUYING AND SELLING SHORT

🔑 Keywords: Discounting, Credit Risk, Buy-Side, Sell-Side, Shorting, Financing

1. Bond Pricing = Discounting Cash Flows

❌ Can't just ADD all cash flows — they occur at different times (TVM!).
✅ Discount each cash flow to today, then add.

Discount factor (year t) = 1 / (1 + r)t
MINI SUMS ✅ (r = 4%)

$1,000 in 1 yr → PV = 1000/1.04 = $961.54 (factor 0.9615)
$3,000 in 2 yrs → PV = 3000/(1.04)² = 3000/1.0816 = $2,773.67 (factor 0.9246)

2. Actual Bond Pricing ⭐

Price = Σ C/(1+r)t + F/(1+r)T C = coupon, F = face value, r = market/discount rate, T = maturity
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅ (Bond PQU)

Face $1,000,000 | coupon 4.5% annual | maturity 5 yrs | market rate r = 5%

  1. Coupon C = 1,000,000 × 4.5% = $45,000 per year (yrs 1–4), year 5 = 45,000 + 1,000,000 = $1,045,000
  2. Price = 45,000/1.05 + 45,000/1.05² + 45,000/1.05³ + 45,000/1.05⁴ + 1,045,000/1.05⁵
  3. = 42,857.14 + 40,816.33 + 38,872.69 + 37,021.61 + 818,784.85
  4. Price ≈ $978,352.62 → trading BELOW PAR (less than $1M)

Why below par? Our bond pays only 4.5% but the market gives 5%. Nobody will pay full price for a weaker coupon → seller must discount. (Math reason: discount rate > coupon rate.)

GOLDEN INVERSE RULE: Interest rates ↑ ⟹ Bond prices ↓ (and vice-versa).
coupon = market rate → AT par | coupon < market → BELOW par | coupon > market → ABOVE par
Fig 3: Inverse relation between bond price & yield

3. Yield to Maturity (YTM)

YTM = the rate x that makes the PV of all bond cash flows equal to the current market price (assuming we hold till maturity).

  • Bond at par → YTM = coupon rate (easy case, e.g., $1,000 bond @ $1,000, 4% coupon → YTM = 4%).
  • Bond BELOW par → YTM > coupon. Example: price $950, face $1,000, coupon 4%, 4 yrs → solve
    950 = 40/(1+x) + 40/(1+x)² + 40/(1+x)³ + 1040/(1+x)⁴ → x ≈ 5.424% (non-linear → use Goal Seek / numerical methods).

Semi-annual coupon bonds (US standard)

Divide coupon rate by 2, divide r by 2, and use 2T periods P = Σ (C/2)/(1+r/2)k + F/(1+r/2)2T, k = 1…2T

General formula (rates/cash flows can vary)

P = Σ CFi / (1 + ri)ti each cash flow discounted at its own maturity's rate — the ri come from ZERO-COUPON bonds

4. Zero-Coupon Bonds ("Zeros")

  • Pay no coupons; sold at a (deep) discount; pay face value at maturity.
  • Used to extract spot rates rt for discounting.
  • Carry market risk + sizable credit risk (issuer may default before paying ANY interest!).
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅

Zero: face $1,000, maturity 10 yrs, price $630. Yield?

r = (1000/630)1/10 − 1 = (1.5873)0.1 − 1 ≈ 4.73% → so r₁₀ ≈ 4.73%

5. Why Corporate Yield > Government Yield?

Treasury 2-yr bond yield 3%, company XYZ 2-yr yield 4%. Same maturity — why different? → CREDIT RISK!

  • Govt = ~zero default risk (can set monetary policy, print money, raise taxes) → its yield = risk-free rate.
  • Company can't print money → some default probability → must offer extra yield to attract buyers → same coupon/maturity bond is cheaper than the risk-free one.
  • Extra yield is NOT free money — defaults do happen!

7. Bid-Ask Spread

  • Bid = price dealer will BUY at (lower). Ask/Offer = price dealer will SELL at (higher).
  • Example: ABC sells bond XYZ @ $101.00 (ask), buys @ $100.50 (bid) → spread = $0.50 = dealer's profit.
AIRPORT KIOSK ANALOGY ✈

Convert €100 → ¥12,800. Flight cancelled, convert back → get only €95. The kiosk sold yen high & bought back low. Do this repeatedly → you go broke, kiosk gets rich! That gap = bid-ask spread.

8. SHORTING (Selling Short) 🔻

Normal trade: buy low → sell high. Shorting reverses the ORDER: sell high FIRST → buy low LATER. But how to sell what you don't own? Borrow it!

5 STEPS OF A SHORT SALE 🔍
① Borrow the security (via broker, from an owner willing to lend)
② Sell it in the market → receive cash
③ Pay fees to the lender (financing cost, grows with time)
④ Buy back later — hopefully cheaper = "covering the short"
⑤ Return the security to the original owner
➜ Profit = Sell price − Buy-back price − financing cost − fees

Margin Account rules (US) 💼

  • Need broker approval for a margin account (borrowing shares ≈ a margin loan; you pay interest on it).
  • Initial collateral (cash/equities) ≥ 50% of the short position. Can't touch the short-sale cash.
  • Maintenance margin ≥ 25% (exchange minimum; broker may demand more).
  • If price RISES → margin call: add collateral, or broker force-buys & returns the shares.
SOLVED EXAMPLE ✅ (Fund ABC shorts MNQ)

MNQ trades ~$80; analysts think fair value $60. Broker locates 500,000 shares, lending rate 5%/yr. Shares sold short @ $79.20. After 6 months price = $72; bought back @ $72.33.

  1. Value of shares borrowed: 500,000 × 80 = $40,000,000 (basis for financing cost)
  2. Proceeds of short sale: 500,000 × 79.20 = $39,600,000
  3. Cost to buy back (cover): 500,000 × 72.33 = $36,165,000
  4. Gross profit: 39,600,000 − 36,165,000 = $3,435,000
  5. Financing cost (compound, 6 months): 40,000,000 × [(1.05)0.5 − 1] = 40,000,000 × 0.024695 ≈ $987,803
  6. Net profit ≈ 3,435,000 − 987,803 ≈ $2.447 million ✔

Financing cost is TWO-fold for bonds!

  • ① Financing fee = interest for borrowing the security.
  • ② Income cost: when the bond pays its coupon, the SHORT SELLER must pay that coupon amount to the original owner (owner holds an "I.O.U." and must not lose income).
Shorting has UNLIMITED loss potential — prices can rise without any ceiling (stocks, Bitcoin!). During the global recession, regulators even banned shorting of financial stocks to restore confidence.

Good side of shorting: stops assets from staying overvalued, adds liquidity, used for hedging. Without shorting, markets can "freeze" (like vacant commercial real estate — ask price ≫ bid price, no deal, property sits empty for years).

9. Short Squeeze 💥

  • Short interest: shares sold short & not yet covered (often % of public float). ↑ short interest = bearish sentiment; ↓ = bullish.
  • Short interest CAN exceed 100% of shares! (Same shares get lent → sold → new owner lends again → shorted again…)
HOW >100% HAPPENS ✅

ABC has 100 shares. Fund TTT owns 50, lends → XYZ shorts them → MNQ buys → MNQ lends the 50 → DEF shorts → KHL buys → KHL lends → RST shorts.
Total shorted = 50+50+50 = 150 → short interest = 150% of available shares!

Squeeze mechanics: heavily-shorted stock price suddenly ↑ → shorts rush to buy back (cover) → huge buying volume → price ↑ MORE → more shorts forced to cover → cascade of price jumps. Plus borrowing costs shoot up → double pain for shorts.

CASE STUDY: GAMESTOP (GME), Q1 2021 🎮
  1. Jan 4: $17.25. Jan 11: Ryan Cohen (10%+ owner) joins board → bullish signal → Jan 13: $38.65.
  2. Short interest hits ~140%! Citron Research mocks retail buyers ("suckers"), then retreats.
  3. Jan 22: $65 → Jan 25: Citadel injects $2.75B into Melvin Capital (big GME short); $76.79.
  4. Jan 26: Elon Musk tweets "Gamestonk!" + r/WallStreetBets frenzy → closes $147.98.
  5. Jan 27: closes $347.51 (touched $380). Jan 28: heading to ~$500 pre-open → apps HALT buying (allow selling) → anger + manipulation suspicion → closes $193.60.
  6. Feb: US Treasury meeting, Congressional hearings; stock falls to ~$40–50; Mar 12 back to $264. (Note: 4:1 split in July 2022, so charts now show ~¼ prices.)
  7. Damage: Melvin Capital lost 30% in Jan alone (later 53% on this one position); D1 Capital lost ~20% (~$4B).
Lesson: shorting is legitimate (price discovery + liquidity) but tough & risky — squeezes can be catastrophic.
LESSON 4

SURVEYING THE FINANCIAL INDUSTRY

🔑 Keywords: Central Bank, Monetary Policy, Investment Banks, Market Risk, Reinvestment Risk, Default Risk

1. Central Banks 🏛

  • Purpose: stability & transparency for the country's economy.
  • Jobs: keep inflation in check, support maximum employment, run healthy monetary policy.
  • Set the rates at which banks borrow/lend from each other → ensures banks always have funds to operate.
  • Ultimate targets: inflation rate & foreign exchange rate.

2. Investment Banks

6 main functions (learn this list!):

  • ① Underwrite securities   ② Help with IPO issuance   ③ Sales & trading with investors
  • ④ Research for clients   ⑤ Bundle & sell securitized products   ⑥ Mergers & Acquisitions (M&A)

They constantly monitor credit risk — of clients, investments, even each other.

Credit scores

FICO score (USA) = Fair Isaac's Company model → one number from 300 to 850 summarizing an individual's creditworthiness. Saves every lender from doing its own full assessment; good credit → cheaper loans.

Maturity buckets & products

BucketDurationTypical products
Short-term< 1 yearCommercial paper, CDs, Treasury bills
Intermediate1 – 10 yearsTreasury notes
Long-term> 10 yearsTreasury bonds

3. Commercial Banks

  • The familiar ones: deposits, credit cards, loans, mortgages.
  • Glass-Steagall Act 1933: forced a strict WALL between commercial & investment banking (JP Morgan had to cut services).
  • Banks dodged via holding companies → Bank Holding Company Act 1956 gave the Fed more oversight.
  • 1999: Glass-Steagall REPEALED → today the distinction barely matters (JPMorgan Chase = huge commercial bank AND top IPO underwriter).

Did the repeal cause the 2008 crisis? (Debate — great for assignments!)

  • YES side: repeal → mergers → giant institutions loaded with mortgage-backed securities → subprime implosion 2008–09.
  • NO side: Lehman Brothers, Bear Stearns, Goldman Sachs were never commercial banks; Countrywide Financial didn't even take deposits. Alternative theory: HUD pushed Fannie Mae & Freddie Mac to buy affordable-housing mortgages → lenders gave loans to people with no down payment / unemployment benefits as income.
  • Countrywide (CEO Angelo Mozilo): pumped out complex mortgages to borrowers who didn't understand or afford them, then packaged & sold them to Wall Street — didn't care what happened after signing. Perfect villain example for risk discussion!

4. Four Risks of Fixed-Income Products ⭐ (compare CD vs Bond)

Default risk is just the EXTREME form of credit risk. Even without default, a mere credit-rating downgrade makes the bond riskier → price drops → you sell for less. Credit risk = degradation of creditworthiness (small scale: score dips; extreme: default).

5. Credit Risk for Lenders = 4 Components

  • ① Fraud risk: is the applicant genuine? (e.g., takes a loan with zero intention to repay — spends it on a vacation! Countrywide barely checked.)
  • ② Default risk: failure to pay. Linked to counterparty risk: A fails to pay B → B fails to pay C → chain/domino of defaults.
  • ③ Credit spread risk: spread of corporate bonds over risk-free bonds reflects perceived risk. Spread WIDENS → asset values fall → losses for lenders/investors.
  • ④ Concentration risk: portfolio stuck in ONE sector/asset class (Countrywide ≈ all real estate!) → one bad event = massive loss; can even become systemic risk (domino across sectors).

6. Exchanges vs OTC Markets

EXCHANGE 🏦OTC (Over-The-Counter) 🤝
StructureCentralized marketplace, well-defined rules for processing & clearingDecentralized — direct deals between counterparties
TransparencyHigh — everyone sees bids, offers, sizes, past tradesLow — different venues may show slightly different prices; hard to know the "fair" price
Credit riskELIMINATED — the exchange takes it! Monitors margins, demands intraday top-ups, can force-liquidateBorne by the counterparties themselves (brokers only facilitate — they take NO credit risk)
ExamplesStocks (trade on one exchange)Many bonds trade OTC
MAMADOU EXAMPLE ✅

Mamadou borrows your security & shorts it. Price jumps ↑ — now your credit risk rises (harder for him to cover). The exchange monitors the value and forces Mamadou to post extra funds (even intraday). Can't post? → forced buy-in, security returned to you. That's how exchanges kill credit risk.

7. Big Picture Recap 🧵

  • Module theme = CREDIT RISK everywhere: loans, deposits, bonds, shorting, financing.
  • Fighters against credit risk: deposit insurance, collateral, regulation, exchanges, central banks.
  • Next up: equities & crypto → main challenge shifts from credit risk to VOLATILITY.
